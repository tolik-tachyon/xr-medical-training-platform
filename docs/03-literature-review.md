# 3. Literature review

## 3.1 Scope and method

- Aim of the review: to identify what is already known about AR/VR training of medical specialists, which approaches exist for feedback and assessment, and which limitations the project must take into account.
- Search sources: Google Scholar and Scopus.
- Search keywords: virtual reality, extended reality, augmented reality, mixed reality, CPR, blood draw, IV, venipuncture, medical procedures, learning analytics, training, simulation, education, software architecture.
- Period: 2021-2026; older foundational works are included only if essential.
- Selection criteria: article, review or conference paper; contains the search keywords; the abstract matches the topic. Sources that are not AR/VR or have no learning outcomes (e.g. [8], [9], [10], [12], [13]) are used as context for platform design, assessment or adoption, and are marked as indirect or partial in the table.
- Result: 13 sources used, all of them scientific publications.

## 3.3 Critical review

### 3.3.1 Effectiveness of AR/VR in medical skills training

The strongest overview is the systematic review [1] (31 studies, 4,926 participants as stated by the authors): immersive VR improved or was non-inferior to conventional training for immediate skill performance in 19 of 23 studies, but the certainty of evidence was low, and very low for retention and satisfaction. Effects were largest against passive comparators and mostly non-inferior against hands-on training. Primary studies agree only partly. The multicenter RCT [5] reported a larger accuracy gain with VR (+21.8 vs +9.4 percentage points), whereas the RCT [11] (246 students) found no difference in self-confidence or group CPR performance, and in [3] the time benefit of AR appeared only after repeated use and most other metrics did not differ. Studies [4], [8] and [9] have no control group, non-randomized groups or knowledge-only and self-reported outcomes, so they show acceptance and knowledge gains rather than skill gains. Overall the evidence is promising but weak: small and single-center samples, short follow-up, mostly nursing students, and few objective measures. VR is therefore treated as a complement to hands-on training, not a replacement.

### 3.3.2 Feedback and objective performance assessment

Real-time feedback is the most consistent mechanism in the sources. The review [6] found that it improves CPR depth, rate, recoil and hand position, mostly for novices, and that human assessment agreed poorly with software-based evaluation. In [5] a shorter feedback delay (38 s to 9 s) mediated skill transfer, and system scores agreed well with examiners (κ = 0.81). The review [7] reports that a summary of errors helped while real-time visualization hurt in one study, so the way feedback is presented matters. Several systems check steps against predefined criteria and require correction before the next step [12], or use checklists and sensors for time, attempts and tool pressure [3], [4], [13]. Assessment is often weak, however: [11] scored CPR only at group level and without inter-rater reliability, students there complained that the VR scenario did not show whether an answer was right, [8] and [9] used self-report and MCQs only, and [13] states that automated performance assessment is still missing.

### 3.3.3 Training of specific procedures (CPR, blood draw / venous access)

For CPR, the evidence comes from the RCT [11], the feedback review [6] and the CPR studies inside [1]. These show that VR can support the CPR algorithm and decision-making, but that headset equipment can impair compression depth and recoil [1], and that depth estimation depends on the input method and sensors [6]. For blood draw and venous access, the sources use AR/MR for ultrasound-guided vascular access [3] and peripheral catheter placement [4], VR with haptic handles and automated scoring for venipuncture [5], a step-checking VR trainer for mouse blood collection [12], and video modules for vascular access and blood sampling [9]. The domain on ultrasound-guided access and arterial blood gas collection in [1] gave the most consistent results. Input methods differ widely (controllers, haptic handles, marker tracking), and marker tracking interfered with natural tool handling in [4]. No source covers CPR and blood draw in one standalone-headset application.

### 3.3.4 Platform design and extensibility

Most sources describe a single custom system for one procedure: [12] for mouse blood collection, [13] for intubation, [5] for venipuncture and [11] for CPR. Their design lessons are still useful. [13] splits the system into modules (laryngoscopy, vital signs, tube insertion) and defines physiological scenarios in JSON/CSV files; [9] organizes content as one module per procedure with a defined scenario (objectives, steps, context, roles); [2] shows that the choice of device can decide whether a procedure can be completed at all. The system in [5] needs a GPU workstation and is hard to reproduce, which argues for a lightweight design. None of the sources describes a common, documented procedure format that allows new procedures to be added without changing the core system.

### 3.3.5 Adoption, usability and educators' perspective

Reported barriers are cost, hardware and technical support, large cohorts, training of educators, and the risk of losing human and relational aspects of care [10], [7]. Learners rated VR as useful and easy to use [11], but 33.6% reported dizziness "sometimes", 19% headaches, and they mentioned heavy headsets, technical problems and cost [11]; simulator sickness is also a listed feasibility issue in [1]. Browser-based and low-hardware solutions lower cost but still depend on bandwidth and device access [8], and high-end setups limit deployment [5]. Educators and reviews agree that XR should complement traditional teaching [10], [7], [1], and that instructor involvement and repeated practice are needed.

## 3.4 Research gap and implications for the project

- Gap: the reviewed solutions are mostly single-procedure systems tied to a specific device or setup, no common extensible procedure format is described, objective step-level scoring is rarely implemented or validated, and the evidence is mostly short-term, small-scale and based on nursing students.
- Implication 1: the project defines procedures in a procedure module with steps, conditions and scoring rules, and adds the second procedure without changing the core system (FR-11, NFR-8).
- Implication 2: the project implements step checking, immediate feedback and per-step data collection, and validates the scoring against guideline values and expert assessment (FR-3, FR-4, FR-8, FR-9, R5).
- Implication 3: the project takes into account input-device limits for CPR depth, comfort, and the lightweight standalone-headset setting (FR-6, NFR-1, NFR-2, NFR-4, R2, R7), and traces every step to a published guideline with expert review (NFR-5, R1, R10).

## 3.5 Summary

Existing work shows that immersive and mixed reality can support procedural training, that fast and objective feedback is the most consistent success factor, and that VR works best as a complement to hands-on practice. The evidence is of low certainty, with small samples, short follow-up, and few objective or validated measures; side effects and cost are recurring barriers. Most solutions cover a single procedure on a specific device, and none describes a common extensible procedure format. This review therefore shapes the requirements for step guidance, immediate feedback, per-step scoring and data collection (FR-2 to FR-10), an extensible procedure module (FR-11, NFR-8), and a lightweight, comfortable prototype (NFR-1, NFR-2, NFR-4). It also shapes the verification plan, which compares sessions against guideline reference values and expert assessment (Section 9).
